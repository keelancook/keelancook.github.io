For many of us, there comes a moment. That moment, whatever it is, whatever God chooses to use, creates a spark and a passion. It's a moment of inspiration and an eye-opening realization that changes the way you see the world. These moments get a person thinking about options, asking questions, and searching out possibilities to be part of God's solution.

I can recall some of these moments in my life. Perhaps you can too.

As the director of our missions center at Southeastern, I have the privilege of working directly with a lot of missionaries. In God's kindness, he's allowed our school to train over a thousand missionaries in the last two decades.

## The Passion

Every semester, I have dozens of students on campus or at a distance actively pursuing a calling to take the gospel where it is not named. It's the joy of my life to serve God and His church in this way.

That means I get to see that moment in others all the time. My weeks are filled with students floored by God's grace through the gospel to them. They can't get over it. Some are clearly burdened by the lack of gospel access in many places around the world. These students find it unacceptable that someone could be born, live her entire life, and die never hearing about this free gift of grace.

That is passion that I see. It is a passion for the Great Commission.

For many of them, it happened on a short-term trip. Some caught it at the conference where they first heard about unreached peoples around the world and the billions (yes, billions) who exist that way. For others, it was a mentor that changed their life and their passions.

[![Dangerous Calling](images/promo/dangerous-calling.png)](https://amzn.to/3SBr4tP){:target="_blank"}

I see passionate people all the time. But there is another side to the coin that I also experience in my role. I have lots of conversations with the missionaries already serving on the field.

A few weeks ago, I ran into a missionary at a meeting. He came over to thank me for a recent episode of our podcast that dealt with hardship on the field. He began to share about his family's last year on the field. Over the course of the year, the family had experienced severe illness, especially the little children, the kind of illness that lands you in emergency care at the hospital. It was the kind of illness that causes you to question if your kid is actually going to make it.

It was a heavy situation, but they were managing. Despite the difficulty, they were pressing through it until they woke one night to find their small child's room on fire. Faulty electrical wiring is not uncommon in developing countries, and it caught their home on fire. Thankfully, their little girl was in their room that night because of her health issues. They quickly escaped their home as it burned down. Needless to say, the family was stateside working with member care and trying to put the pieces of their life back together.

As his story of the recent past caught up to the present, he naturally turned to what was next. After the trauma and loss this family experienced, you might think that conversation revolved around an off-ramp.

But that's not where it went.

The conversation merely moved into discussing their search for a new ministry location on the field. After the setback, they were making plans to get back to the work. Not in that house of course, but on the field with hand to plow.

I'm convinced it takes more than passion to ground the kind of perseverance through hardship exhibited by this family. To be clear, Jesus is not against passion. He is not against excitement for the mission. It is not my job at the seminary to squash that before students go to the field. If anything, my job is to fan that passion into genuine commitment. If you are a pastor, that is your job, too.

Passion is a really good thing, but when Christ tells us to go and make disciples of all nations, He is after more than our passion. That raises a question: Is there something more than passion that makes the difference between the one who lasts and the one who doesn't? What is the difference between the one who produces healthy fruit on the field and the one who sows discord, division, or false doctrine?

## The Foundation

A task as magnificent as the mission of the church requires more than a passion to see it done; it requires a foundation that will last a lifetime. I am convinced we find grounding for this truth in Scripture.

In Luke 6:46–49, Luke records the concluding words of Jesus' Sermon on the Plain. It is a large didactic block of Jesus' teaching, and it is an intense discipleship lesson. In his commentary on Luke, Joel Green refers to this conclusion as the "measure of a disciple."\*

> "Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and not do what I tell you? Everyone who comes to me and hears my words and does them, I will show you what he is like: he is like a man building a house, who dug deep and laid the foundation on the rock. And when a flood arose, the stream broke against that house and could not shake it, because it had been well built. But the one who hears and does not do them is like a man who built his house on the ground without a foundation. When the stream broke against it, immediately it fell, and the ruin of that house was great." —Luke 6:46–49

The passage above is one of two illustrations that Jesus uses to make his point. Five different times between verses 43 and 49 Luke uses the word "do" in some form. These twin illustrations, the tree and its fruit and the builders and their houses, begin and end with doing. Be careful, however. This passage is not calling for some form of behaviorism. The focus is not the actions; the focus is the kind of heart which produces the actions. Jesus concludes this sermon with a matter of character that is formed, shaped, and cultivated in the heart of a person.

The first illustration concerning the tree and its fruit is likely the more familiar passage. There are two kinds of trees, two kinds of fruit. In the next illustration, Jesus discusses two kinds of builders. One builder prepares a foundation that weathers whatever comes; the other builder does not. One builder produces a house that stands; the other builder does not.

## The Question

Jesus does not open this last illustration with immediate instruction. He begins with a question. In fact, it is a pointed question.

> "Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do what I tell you?"

Let that question sit with you for a moment. Coming off the conversation about good and bad fruit, Jesus starts with a question that cuts at least some of his audience as not doing what he says. Note the tension in that question.

Commentators widely agree on the emphasis created by repeating the word "Lord." It is an enthusiastic repetition. Ken Hughes calls it an "intense affirmation of allegiance."\* Leon Morris claims, "To repeat the address is to put certain emphasis on the admission."\* In other words, this is a passionate confession about Jesus.

Jesus immediately follows that passionate statement with a question about follow-through. Of course, people that would use such a designation for Jesus do so to register their enthusiasm, their passion, and that Jesus was the one who had captured their allegiance. Yet for those whom Jesus addresses here, there is no follow-through on their passionate declaration.

We see this happen. In fact, we may be guilty of it. I watch students fall into this tension: real passion with no follow-through.

Furthermore, the passion is real. It is genuine excitement. One goes on a trip somewhere, and it blows his mind. Another sits under a sermon about global lostness that wrecks her. A third student has a mentor who changes his life, and now he wants to be that for someone else. The spark is real, and it is good, but that does not automatically lead to obedience. Left to itself, passion makes a weak foundation.

Think of passion like gasoline. Gasoline is good and essential, and your car does not run without it. Even so, gasoline is not the engine. Good gasoline in a bad engine will not get you very far.

In Luke 6, Jesus's question points to that tension and reveals the difference between some thin, stated allegiance and a thick, genuine commitment to Jesus. Jesus addresses a person's character, the substance of their heart. The illustrations about the trees and the builders showcase people whose actions reveal what is in their hearts. Jesus is not merely asking about what you do; he's asking who you are.

There are many who call him, "Lord, Lord." The question is not whether a person is passionate. The question, as Jesus reveals, is about what a person builds their life upon. Jesus is clear that everyone who hears his words *and does them* is like someone who digs deep and lays a foundation. Passion can come in a moment, but the foundation that lasts requires the slow and steady work of spiritual formation. It is a Word-soaked, prayer-driven, community-oriented process that lays the groundwork for a lifetime of obedience. When that kind of foundation meets passion, then and only then, are the results total and glad surrender.
