Several months ago, I decided to go on a diet. 

I will tell you what did it. I saw a picture of myself on one of our school mission trips. It was the required tourist shot where you stand in front of a famous monument. Everyone has to get one, so I took my turn and let a student snap the shot with my phone. Somehow, it landed on our digital picture frame at the house, and I saw myself. I had not realized how much weight I had gained, and it sank in that this is not just a looks issue but a health issue. 

I need you to understand just how much I hate "health food." I was not certain I could do it. I have tried diets before, and I can never get them to stick. Nevertheless, I traded potato chips for cans of tuna. Here we are months later, and I still do not love a can of tuna, but I've learned to make it a regular meal. And you know what, it worked. I lost the weight, and honestly I feel better, a lot better. The discipline to consume the right food has made my life better, enough better that I don't want to stop. I want to do what it takes to keep the discipline of consuming food that is actually good for me.

This article is not about dieting, but I do believe there is a parallel to an important principle in our spiritual formation and ministry preparation. In a previous article I made the claim that [passion makes a weak foundation for ministry](https://keelancook.com/passion-makes-a-weak-foundation). In this article, I want to add a second condition: "snack food" preparation will not survive the field of ministry.

For this article, I want us to look again at end of Luke 6. Jesus closes his Sermon on the Plain with a picture of two builders. One dug deep and laid a foundation on the rock. The other built straight on top of the ground with no foundation at all. Both houses eventually faced the same flood, yet only one house remained.

> "Everyone who comes to me and hears my words and does them, I will show you what he is like: he is like a man building a house, who dug deep and laid the foundation on the rock. And when a flood arose, the stream broke against that house and could not shake it, because it had been well built. But the one who hears and does not do them is like a man who built a house on the ground without a foundation. When the stream broke against it, immediately it fell, and the ruin of that house was great." (Luke 6:47–49, ESV)

Jesus frames this pericope in verse 47: "Everyone who **comes to me** and **hears my words** and **does them**, I will show you what he is like." Notice the participles. There are three words here that outline with specificity the ideal builder. This person comes to Jesus, hears his words, and acts on them: coming, hearing, and doing. These three together are the structure and theological load-bearers for this entire passage. However, it is a mistake to separate these three verbs into three different ideas.

Notice how Jesus uses these participles together. He's not listed three separate activities; he's describing one whole person. This is what it looks like for your whole person to be oriented toward Jesus. This person hasn't just shown up excited ([passion only](https://keelancook.com/passion-makes-a-weak-foundation)), they have allowed what they heard to move all the way into who they are and what they do.

Jesus draws our attention to two kinds of builders, two kinds of people. But the people are not the only thing in view here. These people are building a house. There is a finished product, a mission they are attempting to accomplish, if you will. They have a task, an assignment, and it will produce something that needs to stand and last.

## Luke Focuses on Preparation
If you are familiar with this story, you are aware Matthew includes it in his gospel as well. In fact, that is likely the more common place to hear it preached. In Matthew's account at the end of the Sermon on the Mount, he distinguishes between these two men based on where they build their house. One builds on the rock and the other on the sand. The focus in Matthew's telling is the ground itself.

Luke's emphasis is slightly different. Luke does not merely emphasize the ground; he focuses on what the builder does with the site. In Luke, it is not singularly about geography; it is about formation. In this passage, formation is accomplished through the coming, hearing, and doing of the whole person toward Jesus.

Look again: 
>...the one who does these things is like a man building a house, *who dug deep and laid the foundation* on the rock. But the one who hears and does not do them is like a man who built a house on the ground... without a foundation.

In his commentary, James Edwards explains a bit of background to this passage. He notes that soil is often "hardpan" in the Levant. That means it is hard, packed dirt. It feels sturdy, and it is terribly hard to dig through. It takes a lot of work. Perhaps more importantly, hardpan soil has the appearance of being stable, of bearing weight.[^1] So a person is tempted to just build on top of the soil with no preparation.

Of course, the short-term gains of building directly on the hardpan soil eventually give way to the long-term problems of having no real foundation. The wise builder resists the temptation for shortcuts and prepares the ground. Leon Morris puts it well:
> "This is essential for sound building, but it is time-consuming and it is hard work. So some avoid it. But when the storms and floods come, a house built on rock will stand. The hard work is worth it."[^2]

So the call at the end of Jesus' sermon is to first dig deep into coming, hearing, and doing. It is a warning that preparation matters for the mission. How we build our lives for the mission we have been given determines how we will stand.

When I lived in Africa, I often heard it said by the locals there that you can go fast or you can go far. It's true in a race, and it is true in our own preparation for the ministry that God has entrusted to us. The more important the work, the more critical the preparation. What more important work is there in all the world than the Great Commission?

There is a house to build: it's the life of a disciple, of a minister of the gospel, of a missionary. How we build that life and ministry determines how it stands.

## Come, Hear, Do — Heart, Head, Hands
The call is to dig deep and spend that front time in preparation for what is ahead, but what does that preparation include?

Here is where I want us to really focus on those three participles. Coming to Jesus, hearing his words, and doing what he says. We come with our hearts, we hear his word and wrestle with our minds, and we do with our hands. It's a whole life, whole person preparation.

This preparation is emotional preparation that grows our affections for Christ, for one another in his church, and for the lost. It is theological preparation that studies to show ourselves approved. It is ministry preparation that lives, daily, in obedience to the Great Commission. That is what digging looks like.

Of course, the other option is just accepting the ground as is. Perhaps you look at your life and see that hardpan soil and think it will hold. Besides, the work is urgent, and you want the fruits of ministry now, so you build your life and ministry without any tilling or digging, without disturbing what already exists. Jesus has severe words for this approach.

## Ground as Secular World
I think our knee-jerk reaction to this passage is to interpret the ground as the secular world around us. We envision its folly, based on a humanist worldview, awash with progressive thought and anti-Christ sentiment. I think that's obvious. We know that ground is unstable. We all know that the missionary who acquiesces to the spirit of this age is destined for destruction.

While that's true, I believe there is a more subtle, and perhaps even more treacherous, implication from this passage. There is another ground that I think *looks stable* but does not provide the sure foundation a missionary needs. It tempts us not to do the real digging.

Perhaps more treacherous than obvious worldliness is mistaking the pop conversations inside our own tribes for the true and good foundation of Christ and His Word. In a media-saturated age, it is so easy to mistake surface teaching or internet scuttlebutt for deep theological truths and real formation.

## Snack Food vs. Whole Food
Now, I move back to discussing diets. The easiest analogy I can provide here is the difference between snack food and whole food. It is easier and easier today to subsist on a diet of theological snack food instead of real biblical substance.

Here's what snack food might look like in missionary preparation:
- I listen to some missions podcast, so I'm now prepared.
- I've got my favorite YouTube sermons or commentators, and that's really where I focus my preparation is through surfing down the algorithm rabbit hole.
- I went to a big conference and heard a guy talk about everything wrong with international missions for 45 minutes, so now I'm an expert.
- I read a blog post criticizing field missionaries, so now I'm trained in what not to do.
- I sat through a lunch panel, and that gave me the insight I needed to do healthy missions.

Before you think I'm being critical of bloggers and podcasters, I am both. I cohost our missions center podcast at Southeastern, and you are presently reading this article on my website. For lack of a better term, it's a blog post. Furthermore, I love doing both. I am not opposed to these kinds of resources. As supplement to deep formation, thin resources like blogs and podcasts are well and good. Potato chips often pair well with a good entrée. That does not mean you should make a meal out of an entire bag of chips.

When it comes to spiritual formation for missions, even a regular diet of thin resources is still a diet of snack food preparation, present website included. Increasing the quantity of blog posts or podcasts does not change what they are. It just means filling your formation with even more thin resources. They are no replacement for genuine, thick theological formation and ministry preparation. Digging deep and laying a true foundation for mission and ministry requires the hard work of serious preparation. It is not snack food you can just grab out of that mobile vending machine we all have in our pocket.
